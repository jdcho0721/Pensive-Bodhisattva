Resonance of Silence

Exhibition Introduction

A tactile and voice-interactive exhibition for the blind and hearing-impaired: Resonance of Silence

An exhibition for everyone, touching the statues — a dialogue between two statues completed by fingertip: the Pensive Bodhisattva and The Thinker.

Exhibition Dates and Venue

Sep 10 (Thu)–Sep 12 (Sat), 2026, World Heritage Industry Expo · Gyeongju HICO Convention Center
Sep 20 (Sun)–Sep 21 (Mon), 2nd floor lobby, National Assembly Members' Office Building

Why This Exhibition Is Different

Most museum exhibitions are designed on the premise of "seeing." This exhibition begins from the opposite premise — it is designed around tactile replica statues, voice narration, and a touch-based AI chatbot, so that blind visitors can complete the visit on their own, without a screen reader and without a companion.

Placing the Eastern Pensive Bodhisattva and the Western Thinker side by side, the exhibition is organized into two rooms comparing two attitudes: a "difficult mind" and a "warm mind."

Room 1 · Encounter

Touching replica bronze statues of Rodin's The Thinker and the Pensive Bodhisattva (50 cm tall) directly with both hands, visitors first grasp form and posture, then meaning through voice narration. The narration uses a pyramid structure, giving the whole picture first, then detailed explanations of each part.

Room 2 · Contemplation and Embodiment

Touching a part of either statue (90 cm tall) — forehead, hand, foot, and so on — with a fingertip plays a voice explanation of that spot's meaning, and any further questions can be asked freely to an AI chatbot. Visitors can also mirror the statues' poses, experiencing the work with the whole body, not just the fingertips.

Room 2 Artwork Introduction — Meaning and Contemplation, Part by Part

Title — Resonance of Silence: Contemplation and Embodiment

This exhibition extends Donald Norman's three levels of emotional design (visceral, behavioral, and reflective) into three aesthetic stages through which the visitor comes to feel a connection with the artwork: Encounter, Embodiment, and Yeoil (Oneness). Encounter (an emotional approach) uses a multisensory interface combining sight, touch, and hearing to convey the intense sensory impact of the visitor's first meeting with the artwork. Embodiment (a behavioral approach) lets the meaning of the artwork naturally soak into the visitor's own body and actions, through physical interaction — tracing the statues' forms and parts with the fingertips and imitating them with one's own body. Yeoil (a reflective approach) brings past and present ways of thinking together as one (如一), completing a time of reflection filled with deep inner calm and inspiration.

Room 2 is not about the overall form, but about tracing, part by part, the meaning and contemplation held in each spot. Touching the same kind of place — brow, hand, posture, foot — on the Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva (Maitreya) and on Rodin's The Thinker, you discover how differently each statue has written its mind into that one spot.

In The Thinker, an unreleased weight is condensed into the brow, the clenched fist, the elbow, the tensed muscles of the back — moments of what Spinoza called conatus, the basic effort of a being to persist in its own existence, still colliding forcefully with the outside world. In the Pensive Bodhisattva, that same weight has been set down at the ear, the smile, the fingers, the folds of drapery, the seated pose, the toes — moments that have slipped free of affliction and touched an unconditioned "joy of cessation." Like the two halves of the old saying "do all you can, then leave the rest to heaven": Rodin's toes, still gripping the ground, are the "doing all you can" that hasn't yet let go; the Bodhisattva's toes, fully at ease, are the "leaving it to heaven" that already has.

Each time you touch a single part in Room 2, this difference between the two minds will reach your fingertips.

Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva (Maitreya)

A Buddhist sculpture estimated to have been made during the Three Kingdoms period (6th-7th century), depicting Maitreya, the bodhisattva who will save all beings in the future, absorbed in deep contemplation. It is notable for the half-lotus posture, right foot resting on the left knee, and fingers lightly touching the cheek. The two most representative examples, held by the National Museum of Korea, were once designated National Treasure No. 78 and No. 83, respectively; today they are labeled simply as National Treasures, without numbers.

Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva (Maitreya) — Artist Unknown

Believed to have been made by the hand of a Three Kingdoms-period artisan, but the artist's name has not been passed down.

The Thinker (Le Penseur)

First created around 1880 by the French sculptor Auguste Rodin as a figure atop The Gates of Hell, then recast in 1904 as an independent, large-scale bronze. A masterpiece of modern sculpture expressing, through tensed muscle and a hunched posture, the difficult mind a person carries through life.

Auguste Rodin (1840-1917)

French sculptor. Left behind works such as The Burghers of Calais and The Gates of Hell, and is credited with opening a new chapter in modern sculpture through the realistic rendering of the human body and rough surface texture.

How to Use

① Entering — Tap the first screen once, lightly, to begin right away (it also starts automatically after one minute if you don't tap). Use the Music button on this screen to turn background music on or off before starting.

② Viewing — Touch (click/tap) any part you're curious about on the statues on screen, and a short explanation of that part plays immediately in voice and captions — touching is the one exception that responds on a single tap. When using a phone camera to recognize your hand instead of a physical statue, the vibration rhythm the moment your fingertip reaches a part tells you its rough shape — short and dense means a rounded, clustered part (like a fist), a long continuous buzz means an elongated part (arm, ear, robe fold), two very short pulses means a narrow, pointed part (finger, toe), and a slow, gentle wave means a broad, flat part (forehead, face). Press the 💬 button on the right-center of the screen to open the "Contemplation Counsel" chatbot (you can also press and drag this button to move it wherever you like). Tap a ready-made suggested question to ask without typing, or type or use the microphone button to ask freely about the part you just touched or either statue. Personal questions like worry or life are answered side by side, in each statue's own voice — if you're currently touching a part, the answer draws on that part's story, and this is not historical fact but a creative interpretation voiced by the two statues. Asking "summarize what I've touched so far" gives you your record so far, plus a rough direction (like "upper left, on the Rodin side") toward any part you haven't touched yet.

③ Playback controls — If you missed an explanation while focused on the touch itself, you can always hear it again by touching the same part again or pressing the Replay button at the bottom of the screen. Most other buttons (Certificate, Serenity, Room 1, 💬, etc.) speak their name and glow gold on the first press; pressing the same button again within a few seconds actually activates it. The ⓘ info button, the Speed and Easy sentences buttons, and the background music volume slider respond immediately on press or movement, with no second press needed.

④ Moving elsewhere — Press the Certificate button (twice) any time to see your record of what you've encountered so far. Pressing the Print button on the certificate screen twice saves it as a PDF file. Pressing the Serenity button (twice) moves you to a mind-clearing screen — after listening to the guidance through to the end, you must press either the Room 1 or Room 2 button shown on that screen yourself to actually move. Pressing the Room 1 button at the bottom (twice) skips this and goes straight to Room 1 any time. The chat panel closes when you press the ✕ button at its top, or by swiping from left to right. This info panel returns one step at a time — from a detail screen back to the menu, or from the menu back to the exhibit screen — when you press the Return button at the bottom, or swipe from left to right. If you're using a keyboard, Esc also closes either the info panel or the chat panel right away.
