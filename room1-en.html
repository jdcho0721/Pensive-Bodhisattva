Resonance of Silence

About This Experience

A mobile audio-description experience for blind and deaf viewers: Resonance of Silence

Audio description for everyone — a dialogue between two statues, the Pensive Bodhisattva and The Thinker, to be enjoyed like watching a film.

An audio-described experience you can complete with just a phone. Meet National Treasure No. 83, the Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva (Maitreya), and Rodin's The Thinker by tapping their images on screen and listening to narration and captions.

Why This Experience Is Different

Most art appreciation is built on the premise of "seeing." This experience begins from the opposite premise — it is designed around spoken audio description, captions, and a touch-based AI chatbot, so that blind viewers can enjoy it from start to finish on their own, without a screen reader and without a companion.

Placing the Eastern Pensive Bodhisattva and the Western Thinker side by side, the experience is organized into two parts comparing two attitudes: a "difficult mind" and a "warm mind."

Part 1 · Encounter

With the images of Rodin's The Thinker and the Pensive Bodhisattva side by side on screen, you follow the audio description as if watching a film, grasping first the form and posture of the two statues, then their meaning. The narration uses a pyramid structure, giving the whole picture first, then detailed explanations of each part.

Part 2 · Contemplation and Embodiment

Tapping a part of either statue image on screen — forehead, hand, foot, and so on — plays an audio description of that spot's meaning, and any further questions can be asked freely to an AI chatbot. You can also mirror the statues' poses, experiencing the work with your whole body.

Part 1 Artwork Introduction — Form and Posture

Title — Resonance of Silence: Encounter

This experience extends Donald Norman's three levels of emotional design (visceral, behavioral, and reflective) into three aesthetic stages through which the viewer comes to feel a connection with the artwork: Encounter, Embodiment, and Yeoil (Oneness). Encounter (an emotional approach) uses a multisensory interface combining sight, hearing, and on-screen touch to convey the intense sensory impact of the viewer's first meeting with the artwork. Embodiment (a behavioral approach) lets the meaning of the artwork naturally soak into the viewer's own body and actions, through physical interaction — tapping parts of the statue images to hear about them, and imitating their poses with one's own body. Yeoil (a reflective approach) brings past and present ways of thinking together as one (如一), completing a time of reflection filled with deep inner calm and inspiration.

Part 1 is a time for picturing the overall form and posture of both statues through audio description, before learning what any single part means. Placed side by side, the Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva (Maitreya) and Rodin's The Thinker let you sense, from silhouette and seating alone, how differently the two figures sit.

The Thinker rests his right elbow on his left knee, his whole body curled tightly forward. Tensed muscle draws the body into a narrow, compact knot, revealing sharp, forceful contours. The Pensive Bodhisattva sits in the half-lotus posture, right foot resting lightly on the left knee, its slender, soft body leaning easily to one side. Its gentle, continuous curves are a sharp contrast to The Thinker. Both share the same "seated in thought" pose, yet split into two entirely different bodily languages: a clenched, curled-in tension and a relaxed, open lean. That contrast is the first thing you discover in Part 1.

Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva (Maitreya) (金銅彌勒菩薩半跏思惟像)

A Buddhist sculpture estimated to have been made during the Three Kingdoms period (6th-7th century), depicting Maitreya, the bodhisattva who will save all beings in the future, absorbed in deep contemplation. It is notable for the half-lotus posture, right foot resting on the left knee, and fingers lightly touching the cheek. The two most representative examples, held by the National Museum of Korea, were once designated National Treasure No. 78 and No. 83, respectively; today they are labeled simply as National Treasures, without numbers.

The Pensive Bodhisattva — the summit of Korea’s bronze-casting tradition

If Rodin was compared to Michelangelo for pushing past the conventions of modern sculpture, the Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva is counted among the greatest masterpieces of East Asian Buddhist sculpture of the sixth and seventh centuries. The pensive form began in India and passed through China, where such figures usually served as attendants beside a main image; Three Kingdoms artisans instead raised the pose into an independent gilt-bronze icon nearly a meter tall. In the painter Sam Gilliam’s words, “It’s not about making something new, it’s about making something your own” — and the Pensive Bodhisattva is the clearest case of a borrowed form made fully Korea’s own.

That delicacy did not appear from nowhere. In the Bronze Age, the finely lined bronze mirror (Danyu Semungyeong) carried more than 13,000 lines spaced about 0.3 millimeters apart on a disc just over 21 centimeters wide — precision hard to reproduce even today. On that tradition, this 93.5-centimeter figure was cast around a single iron core running from head to feet, with walls averaging about one centimeter, and so finely that it shows almost no repairs. Where Chinese pensive figures render the drapery over the seat in a stiff, formulaic way, the folds here are praised for flowing like real cloth. The fingers hovering just short of the cheek are the lightest moment hard metal can hold.

The wooden Pensive Bodhisattva of Kōryū-ji in Kyoto resembles this figure down to the crown and the modeling of chest and waist, and is treated as a leading example of how sculpture from the Korean peninsula carried across the sea to Japan. Japan carved the pose in wood; this figure was poured in metal. And while Rodin’s Thinker was cast many times from plaster models and spread around the world, the Pensive Bodhisattva is one of a kind.

Mark Rothko believed art could reach “the heights of exaltation as well as the depths of despair.” If The Thinker bears those depths with his muscles, the Pensive Bodhisattva crosses the same thought in the stillness of a faint smile. Rashid Johnson has said that “consciousness can be a medium.” Here the gilt bronze is only the material; the true medium is contemplation itself. As Spencer Finch puts it, rather than revealing truths, this smile creates a situation that reveals the world “in a subtle and complex way.”

Robert Irwin wrote that “seeing is forgetting the name of the thing one sees.” Set aside the names — National Treasure, gilt bronze, Three Kingdoms — and simply listen. In Lygia Pape’s words, an artwork exists “not as a finished and resolved object, but as something that is always present, permanent within people.” This smile has lasted fourteen hundred years not in the metal, but within people.

Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva (Maitreya) — Artist Unknown

Believed to have been made by the hand of a Three Kingdoms-period artisan, but no individual artist's name has been passed down.

The Thinker (Le Penseur)

First created around 1880 by the French sculptor Auguste Rodin as a figure atop The Gates of Hell, then recast in 1904 as an independent, large-scale bronze. A masterpiece of modern sculpture expressing, through tensed muscle and a hunched posture, the difficult mind a person carries through life.

According to the National Gallery of Art, Rodin pushed sculpture beyond previously accepted rules of finish and anatomical correctness; his fiercely expressive treatment of the figure led to comparisons with Michelangelo, and his broken, roughly finished surfaces were compared to the innovations of the impressionists. He modeled his ideas in clay or wax with his own hands, had plaster casts made, and employed specialists to enlarge, reduce, and cast them in bronze. The Thinker thus became a veritable best-seller in his lifetime and is now spread across collections around the world. [14]

Auguste Rodin (1840-1917)

French sculptor. Left behind works such as The Burghers of Calais and The Gates of Hell, and is credited with opening a new chapter in modern sculpture through the realistic rendering of the human body and rough surface texture.

How to Use

① Getting started — On the viewing-order screen (the first screen), tap anywhere on the screen once, lightly, and after the guidance voice plays, you'll start Part 1 right away. The Part 1 · Encounter and Part 2 · Contemplation and Embodiment buttons on that same screen also let you start or move directly (press once to hear the name, press again to activate). Use the Music button at the bottom of this screen to turn background music on or off before starting.

② Viewing — Once started, the narration continues seamlessly from the overall composition introduction through to the detailed explanation of each part. On the screen showing captions, tap the left area to go back to the previous line, or tap the right area to move forward to the next line. As if watching a film, simply follow the two statue images on screen while you listen to the narration. The four dots at the top center of the screen represent four stages — Orientation, Global Composition, Posture and Position, and Dialogue — and tapping any dot jumps straight to that stage.

③ Playback controls — Press and hold the screen, or press the Pause button at the bottom of the screen, to pause or resume at any time. The Replay and Pause buttons at the bottom of the screen, and the Speed and Easy sentences buttons and background music volume slider at the top, all take effect immediately on press or movement. Replay plays the caption you're currently viewing again; Pause stops or resumes playback; Speed adjusts the narration speed; Easy sentences switches to simpler sentences; and background music volume adjusts only the background music (narration volume is fixed separately). Place your phone face down to pause the narration, and pick it up again to continue.

④ Moving elsewhere — The Exit (✕) button at the top right and the Part 2 button both speak their name on the first press, and need a second press or a quick double-tap to actually activate. Exit closes the screen (if closing isn't always possible under mobile-browser policy, the screen goes blank instead); Part 2 moves you to Part 2. This info panel goes back one step — from a detail screen to the menu, or from the menu to the viewing screen — when you press the Return button at the bottom, or swipe from left to right. If you're using a keyboard, Esc also closes it right away.

⑤ Continued in Part 2 — Fingertip exploration of each part, trying the poses, and talking with the statues continue in Part 2.

If you use a screen reader, the artwork narration is read by the built-in voice, and buttons and short notices by your screen reader. Please lift your finger from the screen while the narration plays.
