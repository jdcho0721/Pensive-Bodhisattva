Resonance of Silence

Exhibition Introduction

A tactile and voice-interactive exhibition for the blind and hearing-impaired: Resonance of Silence

An exhibition for everyone, touching the statues — a dialogue between two statues completed by fingertip: the Pensive Bodhisattva and The Thinker.

An exhibition completed by fingertip and ear, beyond the eye. Meet National Treasure No. 83, the Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva (Maitreya), and Rodin's The Thinker, through smaller-than-life tactile replicas and tactile and voice narration.

Why This Exhibition Is Different

Most museum exhibitions are designed on the premise of "seeing." This exhibition begins from the opposite premise — it is designed around tactile replica statues, voice narration, and a touch-based AI chatbot, so that blind visitors can complete the visit on their own, without a screen reader and without a companion.

Placing the Eastern Pensive Bodhisattva and the Western Thinker side by side, the exhibition is organized into two rooms comparing two attitudes: a "difficult mind" and a "warm mind."

Room 1 · Encounter

Touching replica bronze statues of Rodin's The Thinker and the Pensive Bodhisattva (50 cm tall) directly with both hands, visitors first grasp form and posture, then meaning through voice narration. The narration uses a pyramid structure, giving the whole picture first, then detailed explanations of each part.

Room 2 · Contemplation and Embodiment

Touching a part of either statue (90 cm tall) — forehead, hand, foot, and so on — with a fingertip plays a voice explanation of that spot's meaning, and any further questions can be asked freely to an AI chatbot. Visitors can also mirror the statues' poses, experiencing the work with the whole body, not just the fingertips.

Exhibition Philosophy (Humartology)

Grounded in the Humartology philosophy — the fusion of Human, Art, and Technology — this exhibition dissolves the boundary between disabled and non-disabled visitors, realizing an aesthetics of accessibility where everyone can equally access a thousand years of contemplation.

Room 1 Artwork Introduction — Form and Posture

Title — Resonance of Silence: Encounter

This exhibition extends Donald Norman's three levels of emotional design (visceral, behavioral, and reflective) into three aesthetic stages through which the visitor comes to feel a connection with the artwork: Encounter, Embodiment, and Yeoil (Oneness). Encounter (an emotional approach) uses a multisensory interface combining sight, touch, and hearing to convey the intense sensory impact of the visitor's first meeting with the artwork. Embodiment (a behavioral approach) lets the meaning of the artwork naturally soak into the visitor's own body and actions, through physical interaction — tracing the statues' forms and parts with the fingertips and imitating them with one's own body. Yeoil (a reflective approach) brings past and present ways of thinking together as one (如一), completing a time of reflection filled with deep inner calm and inspiration.

Room 1 is a space for feeling the overall form and posture of both statues with your hands, before learning what any single part means. Placed side by side, the Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva (Maitreya) and Rodin's The Thinker let you sense, from silhouette and seating alone, how differently the two figures sit.

The Thinker rests his right elbow on his left knee, his whole body curled tightly forward. Tensed muscle draws the body into a narrow, compact knot, so tracing it by hand reveals sharp, forceful contours. The Pensive Bodhisattva sits in the half-lotus posture, right foot resting lightly on the left knee, its slender, soft body leaning easily to one side. Tracing it by hand reveals gentle, continuous curves — a sharp contrast to The Thinker. Both share the same "seated in thought" pose, yet split into two entirely different bodily languages: a clenched, curled-in tension and a relaxed, open lean. That contrast is the first thing your hands discover in Room 1.

Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva (Maitreya) (金銅彌勒菩薩半跏思惟像)

A Buddhist sculpture estimated to have been made during the Three Kingdoms period (6th-7th century), depicting Maitreya, the bodhisattva who will save all beings in the future, absorbed in deep contemplation. It is notable for the half-lotus posture, right foot resting on the left knee, and fingers lightly touching the cheek. The two most representative examples, held by the National Museum of Korea, were once designated National Treasure No. 78 and No. 83, respectively; today they are labeled simply as National Treasures, without numbers.

Gilt-Bronze Pensive Bodhisattva (Maitreya) — Artist Unknown

Believed to have been made by the hand of a Three Kingdoms-period artisan, but no individual artist's name has been passed down.

The Thinker (Le Penseur)

First created around 1880 by the French sculptor Auguste Rodin as a figure atop The Gates of Hell, then recast in 1904 as an independent, large-scale bronze. A masterpiece of modern sculpture expressing, through tensed muscle and a hunched posture, the difficult mind a person carries through life.

Auguste Rodin (1840-1917)

French sculptor. Left behind works such as The Burghers of Calais and The Gates of Hell, and is credited with opening a new chapter in modern sculpture through the realistic rendering of the human body and rough surface texture.

How to Use

① Entering — On the viewing-order screen (the first screen), tap anywhere on the screen once, lightly, and after the guidance voice plays, you'll enter Room 1 right away. The Room 1 · Encounter and Room 2 · Contemplation and Embodiment buttons on that same screen also let you enter or move directly (press once to hear the name, press again to activate). Use the Music button at the bottom of this screen to turn background music on or off before entering.

② Viewing — Once inside, the narration continues seamlessly from the overall composition introduction through to the detailed explanation of each part. On the screen showing captions, tap the left area to go back to the previous line, or tap the right area to move forward to the next line. Feel free to comfortably touch the replica statues within your reach (both Rodin and the Pensive Bodhisattva are smaller than life size) while you listen to the narration. The four dots at the top center of the screen represent four stages — Orientation, Global Composition, Posture and Position, and Dialogue — and tapping any dot jumps straight to that stage.

③ Playback controls — Press and hold the screen, or press the Pause button at the bottom of the screen, to pause or resume at any time. The Replay and Pause buttons at the bottom of the screen, and the Speed and Easy sentences buttons and background music volume slider at the top, all take effect immediately on press or movement. Replay plays the caption you're currently viewing again; Pause stops or resumes playback; Speed adjusts the narration speed; Easy sentences switches to simpler sentences; and background music volume adjusts only the background music (narration volume is fixed separately).

④ Moving elsewhere — The Exit (✕) button at the top right and the Room 2 button both speak their name on the first press, and need a second press or a quick double-tap to actually activate. Exit closes the screen (if closing isn't always possible under mobile-browser policy, the screen goes blank instead); Room 2 moves you to Room 2. This info panel goes back one step — from a detail screen to the menu, or from the menu to the exhibit screen — when you press the Return button at the bottom, or swipe from left to right. If you're using a keyboard, Esc also closes it right away.
